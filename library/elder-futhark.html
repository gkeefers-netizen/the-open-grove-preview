The Elder Futhark

All twenty-four runes, in full: their history, how to say their names, what the rune poems actually say, and what is modern practice — kept apart, so you can always tell which is which. Researched November 2026.

What it is

The Elder Futhark is the oldest runic alphabet: 24 signs, each standing for a sound and carrying a name that is an ordinary word — cattle, hail, ice, sun. The name "futhark" is a modern coinage from the sounds of the first six runes — f, u, þ (th), a, r, k — just as "alphabet" comes from alpha and beta.

It was in use roughly from the 2nd to the 8th century, among Germanic-speaking peoples in Scandinavia, along the North Sea coast, and across central and eastern Europe. It is older than the Viking Age (conventionally c. 793–1066): by the time the Viking Age began, Scandinavians had already reduced the row to the 16-rune Younger Futhark. Calling the 24-rune row "Viking runes" is a common error; the Vikings wrote with the Younger Futhark.

The 24 runes fall into three groups of eight, called ættir (singular ætt, Old Norse for "family, lineage"). The division into three groups of eight is genuinely old: the 6th-century Vadstena and Grumpan bracteates mark the row off into three blocks of eight with dots. The names of the groups are not from the Elder Futhark period — they come from later Icelandic learned tradition (Cleasby and Vigfússon's dictionary of 1874 records Hagals-ætt for the second group) and from modern rune books. "Heimdall's ætt" and "Freyr and Freyja's ætt" are fully modern labels, and so are the themes assigned to each group. This page uses the names as a study aid and says so.

The order was not perfectly fixed, either. The Kylver stone puts Perthro before Eihwaz and ends with Dagaz then Othala; the Vadstena and Grumpan bracteates have the reverse in both places. The modern standard order is a composite of the two. There was no single "correct" final rune — so do not build a story (for example, "the row ends in inheritance") that depends on one arrangement.

Some 350–400 Elder Futhark inscriptions survive, most of them short: names, makers' formulas, ownership marks, memorials, on portable objects — brooches, weapons, combs, gold bracteates — with fewer on stones. Most scholars derive the runes from a Mediterranean alphabet, Latin or a North Italic variety, adapted around the 1st century CE, probably in Romanised regions along the Rhine. Which model exactly is still debated.

How to read this page

Every rune below has its layers kept apart. The name is the reconstructed Proto-Germanic name, marked with an asterisk (*) — a reconstruction, not a word anyone wrote down. The poems are what the three medieval rune poems actually say: the Old English Rune Poem (probably 8th–9th century), the Norwegian Rune Poem (13th-century manuscript, itself lost, known through a 17th-century copy), and the Icelandic Rune Poem (oldest manuscript around 1500). Only 15 runes appear in all three poems; 8 appear in the Old English poem only, because they dropped out of the Younger Futhark. The modern reading is what today's readers do with the rune — a prompt for reflection, built in the 20th century. It is labelled as modern, every time.

How to say the names

Nobody knows exactly how Proto-Germanic sounded, and the rune names are reconstructions — so what follows is a scholarly approximation, in plain English. Stress the first syllable. Þ (thorn) is "th" in "thin". J is "y" in "yes". Ŋ is "ng" in "sing". Long vowels (marked with a line over them, like ū) are held longer than short ones.

Approximately: FAY-hoo · OO-rooz · THOO-ree-sahz · AHN-sooz · RYE-doh · KEN-ahz (Norse: COW-nahn) · GAY-boh · WOON-yoh · HAH-gah-lahz · NOW-theez · EE-sahz · YAIR-ah · EYE-wahz (or EE-wahz — scholars disagree) · PAIR-throw · AHL-geez · soh-WEE-loh · TEE-wahz · BAIR-kah-nah · AY-wahz · MAHN-nahz · LAH-gooz · ING-wahz · DAH-gahz · OH-thah-lah. The ættir: "EYE-teer". Freyr: "FRAY-er". Týr: "teer", rhymes with "deer".

Do not present any of these as "how the Vikings said it". The Vikings spoke Old Norse and used the Younger Futhark; the Elder names are reconstructed Proto-Germanic, several centuries older than the Viking Age.

The evidence on the ground

A few of the finds that anchor the row in real objects:

  • Vimose comb, Funen, Denmark, c. 160. An antler comb reading harja, probably a personal name — the oldest securely dated runic inscription. (Some older literature dates it to the mid-3rd century.)
  • Øvre Stabu spearhead, Norway, c. 180–250. Reads raunijaz, "tester" — the name of the spear itself.
  • Thorsberg chape, c. 200–260. A bronze sword-chape reading owlþuþewaz / niwajemariz — "Wulþu-þewaz, of good repute".
  • Svingerud / Hole runestone, Norway, 50 BCE–275 CE. Fragments found in 2021–2023 and published in Antiquity in 2025; the oldest datable runestone known, though the wide date range means it does not securely pre-date the Vimose comb.
  • Kylver stone, Gotland, Sweden, c. 400. A limestone slab from a grave; the oldest complete 24-rune row in order, followed by a tree-like sign and the unexplained palindrome sueus, plus a stacked bind-rune of six t-runes and four a-runes — an invocation of Týr and the Æsir.
  • Golden Horn of Gallehus, Denmark, c. 400–470. Ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido — "I, Hlewagastiz Holtijaz, made the horn." The original was stolen and melted down in 1802; it survives in drawings.
  • Vadstena bracteate, Sweden, 6th century. A gold pendant with the full row divided by dots into three groups of eight — the direct evidence for the ættir. It was stolen from the Swedish History Museum in 1938 and never recovered; it survives in drawings and photographs. The Mariedamm and Grumpan bracteates show the same three-group division.
  • Stentoften stone, Blekinge, Sweden, c. 520–700. A transitional inscription in which a single j-rune stands for its own name, "good year" — a clear case of a rune used as a word-sign.
  • Björketorp stone, Blekinge, Sweden, c. 520–700. A curse inscription: the carver conceals "runes of power" and the vandal is doomed to "insidious death".

Freyr's ætt — runes 1–8

1. Fehu ᚠ — F — *fehu, "cattle, movable wealth". Say FAY-hoo. All three poems cover it, and they disagree on the feeling: the Old English poem calls wealth "a comfort to every man" yet says each must share it freely to win honour; the Norwegian poem says wealth causes strife among kinsmen; the Icelandic poem calls it "fire of the sea" (gold) and "the serpent's path". The name is secure — Old English feoh, Old Norse fé, Gothic faihu; English "fee" is the same word. Cattle were wealth that walks: food, trade, survival. Modern reading: movable wealth — what you own and what it can do; abundance and flow, and a warning against clinging to what should move on. Example: the lost Gummarp stone (c. 560–700) read "Haþuwulfar placed three staves: fff" — three f-runes standing for their name, one of the clearest cases of a rune used as a word-sign.

2. Uruz ᚢ — U — *ūruz, "aurochs (wild ox)". Say OO-rooz. All three poems cover it, but they disagree: the Old English poem describes the aurochs at length — proud, great-horned, a savage beast that fights with its horns; the Norwegian poem says úr is slag from bad iron; the Icelandic poem says it is drizzle, "the weeping of the clouds, the ruin of the hay-harvest". The aurochs reading is the best-supported reconstruction (Old English poem + Gothic letter name urus). The last aurochs died in Poland in 1627. Modern reading: raw strength, health, vitality — the energy to act and endure. Example: the Øvre Stabu spearhead (c. 180–250), raunijaz — the u-rune in an early weapon-name inscription.

3. Thurisaz ᚦ — TH — *þurisaz, "giant" (Norse) / "thorn" (English). Say THOO-ree-sahz, "th" as in "thin". All three poems cover it, under two names: the Old English poem calls it þorn, a thorn — "very sharp; for every thane it is evil to grasp"; the Norse poems call it þurs, a giant — "torment of women, cliff-dweller". Which name is older is not known. One confusion to avoid: Thurisaz has nothing to do with Thor (Thor's name is a different word, þunraz). In Skírnismál 36, Skírnir threatens Gerðr: "a þurs I carve for you, and three staves" — a curse cut in runes. Modern reading: defense and the force that clears the way — sometimes by breaking things; a thorn is small and it stops you. Example: the Thorsberg chape (c. 200–260), owlþuþewaz — the þ-rune in one of the earliest inscriptions.

4. Ansuz ᚨ — A — *ansuz, "a god, one of the Æsir". Say AHN-sooz. All three poems cover it, and they hear the name three different ways: the Old English poem calls it ōs, the mouth — "the source of all language, a pillar of wisdom"; the Norwegian poem hears óss, an estuary — "the way of most journeys"; the Icelandic poem names the god outright — "aged Gautr, prince of Ásgarðr, and lord of Valhöll", Gautr being Odin. The word appears in the personal name Asugisalas on the Kragehul spear-shaft. Modern reading: the word spoken well — counsel, a message, a story that lands true. The "divine breath" in some modern books (Odin giving önd, breath, to the first humans in Völuspá) is a modern joining of the Odin and speech readings, not something any poem says about this rune. Example: the Kragehul spear-shaft (c. 450–500), Asugisalas; the Lindholm amulet carries eight a-runes in a row, read by some as eight times ansuz.

5. Raidho ᚱ — R — *raidō, "riding, journey". Say RYE-doh. All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says riding is easy for every warrior in the hall and hard work for the one on a powerful horse over the long roads; the Norwegian poem says riding is worst for horses, and Reginn forged the best sword; the Icelandic poem calls it "the rider's bliss, a swift journey, and the horse's toil". The name is secure (Old English rād, Old Norse reið). Modern reading: movement with a purpose — a journey, a plan carried out, the right order of things. Example: the Vimose comb (c. 160), harja — the r-rune in the oldest securely dated inscription.

6. Kenaz ᚲ — K — *kaunan?, "sore/ulcer" (Norse) / "torch" (English). Say KEN-ahz (Norse form kaun: COW-nahn). All three poems cover it, split: the Old English poem calls it cēn, a torch — "known to every living being by its pale, bright flame"; the Norse poems call it kaun, an ulcer — "a curse to children; death turns a corpse pale". Philologists generally favour kauną ("sore") as the older form; the torch belongs to the English branch. Do not present "torch" as the single original meaning. Modern reading: a torch in the dark — skill learned by doing, the smith's fire and the poet's spark (from the English poem only). Keep the other half in mind: the same heat that lights a hall can fester. Example: the Golden Horn of Gallehus (c. 400–470), ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido — the k-rune in ek ("I") in a maker's formula.

7. Gebo ᚷ — G — *gebō, "gift". Say GAY-boh. Old English poem only — the Norse poems have no stanza for it, because the Younger Futhark dropped this rune. The Old English poem says: "Giving is people's pride and praise, and for every outcast who has nothing else it is help and a living." The X shape combines easily: the Kragehul spear-shaft writes g+a three times as single bind-runes, and a gold bracteate from Sealand says gibu auja, "I give luck/protection" — a wish spelled out in words. Modern reading: a gift given and a gift returned — exchange, partnership, the balance between giving and taking. The gift creates the bond. (The "two forces crossing" reading of the X shape is modern symbolism.) Example: the Seeland II-C bracteate, gibu auja; the Kragehul g+a bind-runes (c. 450–500).

8. Wunjo ᚹ — W — *wunjō, "joy". Say WOON-yoh. Old English poem only — the Norse poems have no stanza, because the Younger Futhark dropped this rune (Proto-Norse lost the initial w). The Old English poem says: "Joy is had by one who knows few troubles, and has prosperity, happiness and a sufficient home." Joy here is modest domestic happiness, not ecstasy. In England, as wynn (Ƿ), the rune entered the ordinary alphabet. Modern reading: the glad heart, harmony after the work is done — fellowship, comfort, a place at the table. Example: the Tune stone (c. 375–530), raised for Woduridaz — the w-rune in an early memorial inscription.

Hagal's ætt — runes 9–16

9. Hagalaz ᚺ — H — *hagalaz, "hail". Say HAH-gah-lahz. All three poems cover it, and they do not all say the same thing: the Old English poem calls hail the whitest of grains — it whirls down from the sky and then turns to water; the Norwegian poem calls it the coldest of grains; the Icelandic poem calls it "cold grain, driving sleet, the snakes' sickness" (a kenning for winter). The name is secure (Old English hægl, Old Norse hagall). The Scandinavian form is an H with one slanting bar; the double-barred ᚻ is Continental, Frisian, and English, never Scandinavian. Modern reading: disruption from outside your control. Hail falls and ruins the crop — no one's fault, and no arguing with it. Example: the Vimose comb (c. 160), harja — the h-rune in the oldest securely dated inscription.

10. Nauthiz ᚾ — N — *naudiz, "need, distress, constraint". Say NOW-theez (also spelled Nauthiz/Naudhiz). All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says need is "tight on the chest, yet it often turns to help and healing for those who heed it in time"; the Norwegian poem says need leaves little choice — "the naked man freezes in the frost"; the Icelandic poem calls it "the bondwoman's grief and a hard lot, and toilsome work". Sigrdrífumál 7 names it: "Need shalt thou mark on thy nails" — one of the few Eddic passages that names a rune in a charm. Modern reading: necessity, the friction that forces invention — doing without, and finding out what you actually need. Example: the Lindholm amulet carries three n-runes in its repeated sequence.

11. Isa ᛁ — I — *īsaz, "ice". Say EE-sahz. All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says ice is "very cold, endlessly slippery", glittering clear as glass, "a floor made by frost, fair to see"; the Norwegian poem calls it "a broad bridge; the blind must be led"; the Icelandic poem calls it "the bark of rivers and the roof of the wave, and a danger to doomed men". The name is secure (Old English īs, Old Norse íss). Modern reading: stillness. Ice stops the river, and sometimes stopping is the only move — waiting without struggling until the thaw. Example: the Einang stone (c. 350–400), "[ek go]dagastiz runo faihido" — "I, Godagastiz, coloured/painted the rune" — the i-rune in an early stone inscription.

12. Jera ᛃ — J — *jēra, "year; a good year, harvest". Say YAIR-ah (the j is a y-sound). All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says "Harvest is people's hope, when God, holy King of heaven, lets the earth give bright fruits" to rich and poor; the Norwegian poem says "Plenty is a boon to men; I say that Fróði was generous" (the legendary Danish peace-king); the Icelandic poem says "Plenty is a boon to men, and a good summer, and a field grown ripe." When Scandinavian lost the initial j, the name became ár and the rune took the value a. Modern reading: the turning year, the harvest after the work — the slow reward. What you sowed comes back, in its season, not yours. Example: the Stentoften stone (c. 520–700): a single j-rune stands for its own name — Haþuwulfar "gave good year". One of the clearest cases of a rune used as a word-sign.

13. Eihwaz ᛇ — EI — *īwaz or *eihwaz, "yew". Say EYE-wahz (or EE-wahz; scholars disagree — say so). Old English poem only. It calls the yew "a tree rough on the outside, hard and firm in the earth, supported by its roots, a keeper of fire, a joy on the estate." The yew is evergreen, long-lived, and its wood made bows. Eihwaz is not the world-tree: Yggdrasil is an ash in the sources (Völuspá 19); the yew-as-world-tree is a modern suggestion. It is the 13th of 24 runes, not the centre of the row. Modern reading: endurance — what bends without breaking. Example: a bone from Caistor-by-Norwich, England (c. 425–475), reading raïhan ("roe deer") — the ï-rune used for a vowel in a real word.

14. Perthro ᛈ — P — *perþ- / *perō?, meaning unknown. Say PAIR-throw. Old English poem only. It says: "Peorð is always play and laughter for the proud, where warriors sit cheerfully together in the beer-hall." Peorð is a word found nowhere else. The most widely accepted reading, going back to Bruce Dickins (1915), is a lot-cup or dice-box — the container the lots were shaken in — and the rune's cup-like shape supports it. But a gaming piece, a pear tree, and other readings have all been proposed, and Dickins himself warned that "no stress can be laid on any of the suggested meanings". "Unknown" is the honest scholarly answer. The reconstructed name itself is a guess. Modern reading: mystery, chance, fate — the cup shaken and the lot cast. "The web of wyrd" and weaving-as-fate imagery are modern. Example: none distinctive — the rune is very rare in actual inscriptions and is attested principally in the rune-rows (Kylver, Vadstena). Say that plainly rather than inventing one.

15. Algiz ᛉ — Z — *algiz? / *alhiz?, meaning uncertain ("elk"? "protection"?). Say AHL-geez (also spelled Elhaz). The Old English poem covers its eolh form; the Norse poems cover its descendant ýr (yew). The Old English poem says eolh-secg ("elk-sedge") "mostly lives in the fen"; it grows in water and wounds cruelly — Dickins concluded it was "some rush, species unknown". The Norse poems say ýr is "the greenest of trees in winter" and "a bent bow". Because the sound /z/ never begins a word, the rune could not be named the usual way. The name algiz ("elk") is a 19th-century convention: Jacob Grimm suggested it in 1821 from the Old English poem's eolh-secg — "elk-sedge", a marsh plant, not an elk standing in a field. Whether the rune was ever named for an elk is debated; alhiz ("protection, sanctuary") has also been proposed. The "protection" reading is modern: readers saw raised arms, or antlers, and made it a rune of defense. No early source calls it protective. Modern reading: protection, and the higher self that watches over you. Example: the Øvre Stabu spearhead, raunijaz (c. 180–250) — the z-rune as a word-final ending, its usual position; the same ending appears in the erilaz ("rune-master") formula on several objects.

16. Sowilo ᛊ — S — *sōwilō, "sun". Say soh-WEE-loh (also Sowelu/Sigel/Sól). All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says "The sun is always a hope to seafarers" as they cross the fishes' bath; the Norwegian poem says "Sun is the light of the lands; I bow to the holy judgment"; the Icelandic poem says "Sun: shield of the clouds and shining disc, and the bane of ice." None mentions the wolf that pursues the sun until Ragnarök. The name is secure (Old English sigel, Old Norse sól). Modern reading: victory, clarity, wholeness — the light after the storm. Example: the Golden Horn of Gallehus (c. 400–470), hlewagastiz — the s-rune in a maker's formula.

Týr's ætt — runes 17–24

17. Tiwaz ᛏ — T — *tīwaz, "the god Týr". Say TEE-wahz. All three poems cover it: the Old English poem calls tīr one of the signs that "keeps faith with princes, holds its course over the night mists and never fails" — probably a star or constellation (Dickins suspected tīr was a misreading of Tīw, the god); the Norwegian poem says "Týr is the one-handed of the Æsir; often the smith must blow"; the Icelandic poem says "Týr: the one-handed god, the wolf's leftovers, the ruler of temples." The Norse poems remember the hand bitten off by Fenrir. Sigrdrífumál 6 links the rune with weapons: carve "winning-runes" on the sword-hilt, "and twice shalt thou call on Tyr". The shape is an upward arrow. Modern reading: justice, courage, victory won honourably. Example: the Kylver stone (c. 400) ends with a stacked bind-rune of six t-runes and four a-runes, read as an invocation of Týr and the Æsir.

18. Berkana ᛒ — B — *berkanan, "birch". Say BAIR-kah-nah (also Berkana/Beorc/Bjarkan). All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says the tree is "without fruit" yet puts out shoots without seed; the Norwegian poem says "Birch has the greenest leaves of any bough; Loki was lucky in his deceit"; the Icelandic poem says "Birch is a leafy branch and a little tree, a young wood." No poem mentions a "silver top" or a birch goddess. The birch is the first tree of spring, pale and quick to grow. Modern reading: growth, renewal, new beginnings. ("Motherhood" and a "birch goddess" are modern additions; no such goddess appears in Norse sources.) Example: the Tune stone (c. 375–530), arbija (inheritance) — the b-rune in an early memorial inscription.

19. Ehwaz ᛖ — E — *ehwaz, "horse". Say AY-wahz. Old English poem only. It says: "The horse is a joy to princes before warriors, a steed proud of its hooves, when wealthy men on horseback trade words about it." The riders exchange words, not horses. The "two horses running together" in some modern books is a modern image; the poem describes one horse. In Scandinavia the word became jór and the rune disappeared. The shape is like an M. Modern reading: trust and partnership — movement made together. Example: the Golden Horn of Gallehus (c. 400–470), ek hlewagastiz... — the e-rune in the opening word ek ("I") of a maker's formula.

20. Mannaz ᛗ — M — *mannaz, "man, human being". Say MAHN-nahz. All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says "A man in his joy is dear to his kin, yet each must fail the other, for the Lord will commit the poor flesh to the earth"; the Norwegian poem says "Man is an increase of the dust; great is the hawk's grip"; the Icelandic poem says "Man: man's delight, increase of the earth, adorner of ships." Mortality dominates all three poems and usually drops out of modern readings. The name is secure (Old English mann, Old Norse maðr). Modern reading: the self seen clearly, and the help we give each other. Example: the Vimose chape (c. 250–300), makija ("sword") — the m-rune in an early weapon inscription.

21. Laguz ᛚ — L — *laguz (or *laukaz, "leek"), "water, lake/sea". Say LAH-gooz. All three poems cover it: the Old English poem says "The sea seems endless to people" who must venture out in a rocking ship while the waves terrify them; the Norwegian poem says "A waterfall is water that falls from the mountain, but ornaments are of gold"; the Icelandic poem says "Water: a welling stream, a wide cauldron, the land of the fish." Some manuscript rune-rows give names pointing to laukaz, "leek" — a formula word on several bracteates — so "leek" is a live alternative for the original name. Modern reading: intuition, feeling, going with the current instead of against it. (The "unconscious" is a 20th-century idea; the poems describe real water.) Example: the Golden Horn of Gallehus (c. 400–470), hlewagastiz holtijaz — the l-rune in a maker's formula; the laukaz ("leek") formula appears on several 5th–6th-century bracteates.

22. Ingwaz ᛜ — NG — *ingwaz, "Ing, a god or hero". Say ING-wahz (also Inguz). Old English poem only. It says: "Ing was first seen among the East Danes, until he later went back over the waves, his wagon running after him." Ing is usually identified with Yngvi-Freyr, the Norse god of fertility — the link is widely accepted, but the poem does not name Freyr, and says nothing about seeds or fertility. Calling Ingwaz a fertility symbol is a modern association, not a historical one. In real words the ng sound was written with a taller sign on a stave, read by some scholars as a bind-rune of i and ŋ; the free-standing diamond form occurs only in the rune-rows. Modern reading: potential held in stillness — the seed in the ground, waiting for its season. Example: attested in the rune-rows (Kylver stone, c. 400; Vadstena bracteate, 6th century). No distinctive standalone inscription in running text is known — the ng sound in words was written differently.

23. Dagaz ᛞ — D — *dagaz, "day, dawn". Say DAH-gahz. Old English poem only. It says: "Day is the Lord's messenger, dear to men, a joy and a hope to rich and poor, useful to all." The shape is two triangles meeting at their points. It is the last rune on Kylver but second-last on the Vadstena and Grumpan bracteates. The name is secure (Old English dæg, Old Norse dagr). Modern reading: the dawn breaking — clarity, awakening, the turning point where dark becomes light. ("Two forces in balance" read into the shape is modern.) Example: the Tune stone (c. 375–530), woduridaz — the d-rune in an early memorial inscription.

24. Othala ᛟ — O — *ōþala, "inherited land, ancestral property". Say OH-thah-lah (also Othila/Odal/Ēþel). Old English poem only. It says: "A homeland is very dear to every man, if he may enjoy there, in his own house, what is right and fitting, in prosperity." It is about a peaceful home, not bloodlines. The shape is a diamond on two legs. Attested in some of the earliest inscriptions (Hole/Svingerud fragments, Vimose planer, Thorsberg chape). One scholar speculated the o-rune on the Thorsberg chape is an ideograph for inherited property — a speculation, not settled. Modern reading: the ancestral land, what is passed down — home, name, belonging; legacy and the ground you stand on. Example: the Thorsberg chape (c. 200–260), owlþuþewaz — one of the earliest attestations of the o-rune.

What is old, and what is new

Runes as writing (2nd–8th centuries). The corpus is memorials, makers' marks, ownership, short messages, names — about 350–400 inscriptions, most of them short. People carved them on stone, wood, bone, and metal. This is the primary, overwhelming use.

Runes used as word-signs. A rune could stand for its own name: the Stentoften j-rune for "good year", the Gummarp fff, the use of ᛟ three times in the Beowulf manuscript for eþel. This is attested — and it is the genuine seed from which later divinatory meanings grew. It is not evidence of an oracle system.

Runes in magic — magic done with writing. The Eddic poems show it: Hávamál 138–139 (Odin hangs nine nights on a windy tree, wounded with a spear, and takes up the runes) and the Ljóðatal 146–163 (eighteen charms; only the twelfth explicitly uses runes, to make a hanged man speak); Sigrdrífumál 5–19 (victory runes on a sword hilt, ale runes for a drinking horn, birth runes, sea runes, healing runes cut in bark); Skírnismál 36 (a þurs carved as a curse); Egils saga (Egil shaves harmful runes off a whalebone and carves healing ones — chapter 72 in modern numbering — and the drinking-horn episode, chapter 44 in Green's 1893 translation, where he carves runes to test for poison). Bracteate formulas such as alu ("ale", function debated) and gibu auja ("I give luck") are written-out wishes. The Björketorp stone carries a curse. None of this is reading the future from drawn runes, and no source gives the individual runes fixed divinatory meanings.

The rune poems. Three medieval poems (plus the short 9th-century Abecedarium Nordmannicum, a mnemonic list of the 16 Younger runes). The Old English Rune Poem: 29 stanzas, probably composed in the 8th–9th century, surviving only because George Hickes printed it in 1705 before its manuscript burned in the Cotton library fire of 1731. The Norwegian Rune Poem: 16 rhyming couplets, from a 13th-century manuscript, itself lost, known through a 17th-century copy. The Icelandic Rune Poem: 16 stanzas of three kennings each, oldest manuscript around 1500, each stanza carrying a Latin gloss. The poems are Christian-era, mnemonic verses about the things the rune names mean — not divination keys.

Tacitus, Germania 10 (98 CE). Germanic peoples cut slips from a fruit-bearing tree, marked them with signs (notae), scattered them on a white cloth, and picked up three, one at a time, to read the answer. Tacitus wrote about sixty years before the earliest securely dated runes and never says the marks were runes. The link between his lots and the runes is a guess. What it shows is that casting lots for guidance is very old among Germanic peoples — older than the runes themselves. It does not support a three-rune rune spread.

Runes were not banned by the church. They went on being carved in Scandinavia well into the Christian Middle Ages — on church walls, baptismal fonts, and gravestones, alongside Latin writing — and survived in marginal use as medieval runes (c. 1100–1500) and Dalecarlian runes (c. 1500–1910). People stopped using them because the Latin alphabet was more practical for writing on parchment, not because anyone outlawed them.

Bind-runes are historical; personal sigil bind-runes are not. Joining runes on a shared stave is old (Kragehul g+a, Tune stone d+a). Merging runes chosen for their divinatory keywords into a personal talisman has no historical precedent. (The Bluetooth logo merges the Younger runes Hagall ᚼ and Bjarkan ᛒ, the initials of King Harald Bluetooth — a modern logo, not an ancient bind-rune.)

What is modern. Johannes Bureus (1568–1652), a Swedish antiquarian, developed a runic system based on the Kabbalah — an early esoteric use, but not the ancestor of today's rune-casting. Guido von List (1848–1919) claimed eighteen runes had been revealed to him in a vision in 1902 and published them as Das Geheimnis der Runen in 1908; his invented 18-rune "Armanen" row, built on the Younger Futhark and on a misreading of Hávamál, supplied meanings that still circulate unattributed, and his system was later absorbed into völkisch and then Nazi symbology. The Armanen runes are not the Elder Futhark. Ralph Blum's The Book of Runes (1982) is the direct ancestor of the practice most people meet today: a bag of ceramic tiles, an order of his own devising, short keyword meanings, upright and reversed draws, and a 25th blank tile he called "Odin's rune". Blum said his interpretations owed much to the Chinese I Ching. The blank rune, the "Wyrd" rune name for it, the reversed (merkstave) readings, and the three-rune spreads all come from this 20th-century line, not from the Viking Age. Blum himself later said the blank rune came from a home-made set and two photocopied sheets he acquired in England in 1976, and called it a "later innovation". Edred Thorsson (Stephen Flowers), Futhark: A Handbook of Rune Magic (1984), and Freya Aswynn, Leaves of Yggdrasil (1990), were more historically engaged, drawing on List and the academic literature — and are the source of many elemental, tree, and deity correspondences now treated as traditional.

A note on misuse

Four of these runes — Sowilo (especially doubled, the "SS bolts"), Tiwaz, Algiz, and Othala — have been used in Nazi and white-supremacist symbolism, and the Anti-Defamation League lists them as hate symbols in that use, while noting their innocent use by pagans. Context decides. The Grove uses them only in their proper context — the full alphabet, the poems, the history — never as a lone emblem without it. If you wear or display them, know what company the shapes have kept.

Further reading

Start with the tradition's own voices and the scholars who study them:

  • Michael P. Barnes, Runes: A Handbook (Boydell Press, 2012). The standard scholarly handbook — clear, sceptical, and readable. Best first book.
  • Tineke Looijenga, Texts & Contexts of the Oldest Runic Inscriptions (Brill, 2003). The scholarly basis for the reconstructed rune names used on this page; detailed readings of the early inscriptions.
  • Bruce Dickins, Runic and Heroic Poems of the Old Teutonic Peoples (Cambridge University Press, 1915). Texts and translations of all the rune poems. Public domain; available free online.
  • R. I. Page, An Introduction to English Runes (Boydell, 2nd ed. 1999/2006). Short, authoritative, and very readable on the English side of the tradition.
  • Samnordisk runtextdatabas / Rundata (Uppsala University). The searchable database of runic inscriptions — the place to check any inscription reading or date.
  • Swedish History Museum (Stockholm) and National Museum of Denmark. Object pages for the Kylver stone, the Vadstena and Grumpan bracteates, and the Vimose finds.
  • Museum of Cultural History, University of Oslo — the Svingerud/Hole runestone; Antiquity 99 (2025), 422–439. The 2025 peer-reviewed report on the oldest datable runestone. Good for showing the research is still moving.
