Norse & Germanic Heathenry

ÁsatrúOW-sah-troo · Heathenry · Norse paganism

Devotion to the Norse gods — Odin, Thor, Freyr, Freyja, Frigg, and many more — and to ancestors and the spirits of the land. Heathenry is built on reciprocity: a gift for a gift. Its wellspring is the Poetic and Prose Eddas and the wisdom poem HávamálHAH-vah-mahl, and its heart is frith — the peace kept within family and community. The old poems are where its images come from: in Grímnismál, Odin's ravens Huginn (Thought) and Muninn (Memory) fly over the world each day and return to whisper him the news — Odin says he fears for Huginn, “but for Munin my care is more.” In Grímnismál, the world-ash Yggdrasil stands ever green above Urth's well — eagle above, the dragon Nidhogg gnawing below, the squirrel Ratatosk running between them — where the three Norns shape every fate. Those two images — the ravens and the tree — are where our newest designs began.

The gods, in their own terms

Heathens speak of two families of gods. The ÆsirAY-seer are the gods of Asgard: Odin the Allfather, Thor with his hammer Mjöllnir, Frigg, Baldr, Tyr, Heimdall, and Loki, whose mischief ends in Baldr's death and his own binding. The VanirVAH-neer are the powers of earth and sea — Njord, Freyr of the good harvests, and Freyja, who takes half the battle-slain to Fólkvangr. The poems tell of a war between them, ended in peace. Most Heathens honor both families.

The gods are not all-powerful and not all-good. They are strong and clever, but fallible — they quarrel, boast, and blunder. They die, too: at Ragnarök, Odin is swallowed by the wolf Fenrir and Thor falls nine steps after killing the world-serpent. Yet the poems do not end there — a new earth rises green from the sea and Baldr returns. Heathenry does not promise that the good win. It promises that the world is worth standing in while it lasts.

How practice actually looks

Most practice is small and plain: a shrine in a corner, a drink poured out. The two rites most people will meet are the blót and the sumbel. A blót is an offering — usually mead, ale, bread, or the first fruits of a garden, poured out and shared. A sumbel is a round of ritual toasts: a horn is passed, and each person speaks before drinking — to a god, an ancestor, or an oath to be kept. Words spoken over the horn carry weight, and nobody is ever pressed to drink alcohol to take part.

Ancestors are honored by name, and the land's wights are greeted on arrival and thanked on leaving. Hospitality is a sacred duty: the Hávamál opens on how to behave as guest and host — feed the traveller, give him a seat and a drink. In Grímnismál, a king starves a guest between two fires for eight nights — and it kills the king, not the guest: Odin reveals himself, and Geirrøth falls on his own sword.

The old stories it draws on

The sources are poems and stories written down in Iceland long after the old faith had ended. The Poetic Edda is a collection of old poems — most from the 900s to the 1200s — written down around the 1270s in a manuscript called the Codex Regius. The Prose Edda is one man's book: Snorri Sturluson wrote it around 1220 as a textbook for young poets, retelling the myths so they could understand the kennings in the old poems. Snorri was a Christian writing two hundred years after Iceland's conversion. Read the two together: the Poetic Edda is the old voice, raw and often broken; the Prose Edda is the handbook that explains it.

The Hávamál is the poem Heathens live by: Odin's counsel — watch your drink, keep your wits, hold your tongue, stay loyal to friends, and remember that a good name outlives you. The runes, too, come from the Hávamál: Odin hangs nine nights on the tree and takes them up, and Heathens use them today for study, meditation, and divination. “Cattle die, and kinsmen die, and so one dies one's self; but a noble name will never die, if good renown one gets.”

Yggdrasil, the Nine Worlds, wyrd, and frith

The world hangs on Yggdrasil, the great ash tree: eagle above, the dragon Nidhogg gnawing below, the squirrel Ratatosk running spite between them. At Urth's well sit the three Norns — Urðr, Verðandi, and Skuld — who shape every fate. Around the tree lie the worlds: Asgard, Vanaheim, Alfheim, Midgard (our world), Jotunheim of the giants, Svartalfheim — also called Nidavellir — of the dwarfs, Niflheim of ice, Muspelheim of fire, and Hel, the realm of the dead. No old source lists all nine in one place; the list is pieced together, and honest books say so.

Wyrd is fate — not a fixed script, but the weave of everything already done, pulling on everything yet to come. Even the gods cannot escape it; the poems' answer is courage, not resignation. Frith is the peace kept within family and community — loyalty, hospitality, kept oaths. A gift for a gift is how frith is made and kept.

What Heathenry is not

There is no central authority — no pope, no high council, no creed every Heathen must sign. Groups, called kindreds or hearths, govern themselves, and many Heathens practice alone. It is a reconstruction, built from old poems, sagas, and archaeology — not an unbroken priesthood. The old faith ended with the coming of Christianity, and modern Heathenry — organized in the later twentieth century — is an honest rebuilding from what survived.

Two things are said plainly here. First, the Vegvísir — the eight-armed stave sold everywhere as a “Viking compass” — is not a Viking-Age symbol at all. Its earliest known appearance is in the Huld manuscript, an Icelandic book of magic compiled in 1860, some eight hundred years after the Viking Age ended. It is later Icelandic folk magic, but calling it Viking is simply wrong. Second, folkish and racialist distortions of Heathenry exist — people who claim the gods belong to one bloodline or one skin color. They are rejected by the inclusive mainstream of the tradition, and by this grove. One caution, too: a pocket Hávamál whose author supports the AFA, and whose proceeds fund it, has been flagged by Norse groups as a white-supremacist publication — do not buy it.

Heathenry is not Wicca: no Wiccan Rede, no Lord-and-Lady duotheism, no circle-casting as a central rite, and the Wheel of the Year is not its core calendar.
