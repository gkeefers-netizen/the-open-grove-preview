Ritual recipes — begin where you stand

No tools to buy, no permission to seek. Each recipe takes about five minutes, start to finish: what you'll need, then the steps in order. Filter by the tradition you'd like to learn from — or keep “All paths” and borrow freely, with respect.

1

Keep a small altar

About 5 minutesGeneral · all pathsCan be done quietly

A shelf, a windowsill, a corner of a dresser. The altar isn't decoration — tending it for two minutes a day is the practice.

You'll need

  • A candle (or LED candle) for fire
  • A small bowl of water
  • One stone, leaf, or shell from a place you love
  • A cloth or tray to gather them on, if you like

Steps

  1. Choose and clear (1 min). Pick your spot and wipe it clean. Clearing the space is the first offering.
  2. Place the elements (1 min). Candle at the back, water beside it, your found object in front — earth, fire, and water in arm's reach.
  3. Light and name it (1 min). Light the candle and say, aloud or silently, what this altar is for: gratitude, ancestors, the season — one sentence is enough.
  4. Tend it (1 min). Straighten, refresh the water, remove anything dead or dusty. Small care, done daily, beats grand ritual done rarely.
  5. Close (1 min). One slow breath, a quiet “thank you,” and snuff — don't blow — the flame out.

Done when the spot feels claimed. Repeat daily; change the found object with the seasons.

2

Learn your land — the sit-spot walk

About 5 minutesCeltic · DruidryGeneralFew supplies

Druids begin with the land they're actually on. Walk the same short route and watch it change — paganism starts with paying attention to the earth under your feet.

You'll need

  • One short route you can repeat — a block, a garden, a park path
  • Your phone or a notebook for three quick notes
  • No headphones

Steps

  1. Set out slowly (1 min). Walk at half your usual pace. Let your eyes go soft and wide.
  2. Name five things (2 min). Five trees, birds, or plants by name if you can — “oak,” “crow,” “clover.” Look up the ones you can't name when you get home.
  3. Read the season (1 min). What's budding, fruiting, falling, returning? Where's the frost, the first flower, the last leaf?
  4. Leave it better (1 min). Pick up one piece of litter, or move one fallen branch off the path. Attention with hands.

Same route, once a week. In a year you'll know your land the way you know a friend's face.

3

Make an offering — a small blótBLOHT

About 5 minutesNorse · HeathenGeneral

A gift for a gift — the first law of the grove. In Heathen practice this is a blót: food or drink given to gods, ancestors, or land spirits, and shared by the people present.

You'll need

  • A cup of something good — ale, juice, coffee, clean water
  • A small bite of food — bread is traditional
  • Your altar, a tree's roots, or a garden spot

Steps

  1. Settle (1 min). Stand at your spot, cup in hand, and let the day go quiet.
  2. Speak the gift (1 min). Say who it's for and why, in plain words: “For the ancestors, in thanks for —” and name one true thing.
  3. Pour and place (1 min). Pour half the drink at the roots or into a bowl on the altar; set the bread beside it. Outdoors, use only things the earth can take — no plastic, no glitter.
  4. Share (1 min). Drink the rest yourself. An offering shared is an offering completed.
  5. Close (1 min). “A gift for a gift.” Leave the food for the birds; pour bowl-water at a plant's roots later.

Small and sincere beats grand and rare. Once a week is a strong rhythm.

4

Honor your dead

About 5 minutesGeneral · all pathsNorseCeltic

Most pagan traditions begin here — the ancestors are the oldest altar most of us have. Say their names, and they stay part of the household.

You'll need

  • One photograph, or an object that was theirs
  • A candle
  • One story about them that you actually remember

Steps

  1. Set the place (1 min). Photo where you'll see it, candle beside it.
  2. Speak the names (1 min). Aloud, full names, one by one — family first, then ancestors of heart: teachers, friends, the ones who raised you in spirit.
  3. Tell one story (2 min). One true story, told well, to whoever's at your table — or to the empty room. Details matter: the laugh, the hands, the saying they always said.
  4. Thank and close (1 min). Thank them for one specific thing that reached you. Snuff the candle.

One story per season is eight ancestors kept alive a year at any table.

5

Full-moon esbatESS-bat — moon water & one intention

About 5 minutesWiccanGeneral

An esbat is the Wiccan observance of the moon, usually at the full moon — the monthly counterpart to the eight solar sabbats. The tracker on the home page tells you the next one. This is its smallest honest form.

You'll need

  • A clean jar or bottle of water
  • A windowsill or doorstep the moon can reach
  • Paper and pencil

Steps

  1. Go out and look (1 min). Find the moon first. Standing under it is step one, not a preliminary.
  2. Set the water (1 min). Place the jar where moonlight will fall on it overnight.
  3. Write one intention (2 min). One sentence, present tense, about what this moon cycle is for: “This cycle, I finish —” Keep it where you'll see it.
  4. Close the circle (1 min). Hands on the jar, one breath, “So it is.” In the morning, drink a sip or water a plant with it — intention, taken in.

Check the next esbat in the today bar on the home page and put it on your calendar now, while you're thinking of it.

6

Keep the turning year

About 5 minutesGeneral · all pathsFew supplies

Eight small observances a year will teach you the shape of the seasons better than any book. This recipe is how you set the next one up — five minutes now, a festival kept later.

You'll need

  • The Wheel of the Year — and the next festival's date
  • Your calendar
  • One simple plan: a meal, a candle, or a sunset walk

Steps

  1. Find the next turning (1 min). Use the today bar on the home page: note the next festival and the next full moon, with dates.
  2. Put it on the calendar (1 min). Both of them, right now — a festival you have to remember is a festival you'll miss.
  3. Choose one small keeping (2 min). Pick a single way from the festival panel on the Wheel of the Year page — the dumb supper at SamhainSOW-in, seeds at Ostaraoh-STAR-ah, bread at LughnasadhLOO-nah-sah. One, done fully.
  4. Tell one person (1 min). “I'm keeping Yule with a candle at dusk — join me?” Festivals grow by invitation.

That's the whole system: look ahead, write it down, keep it small, share it once.

7

Draw one rune for the day

About 5 minutesNorse · HeathenGeneralCan be done quietlyFew supplies

One rune, drawn before the day gets going, is a modern way to set the tone — the old root is Tacitus's lot-casting, and the daily draw is ours. The rune draw on the Wisdom & Runes page works the same way, if you have no set of your own.

You'll need

  • A rune set, or the rune draw on the Wisdom & Runes page
  • A flat surface

Steps

  1. Settle (1 min). Sit down, feet on the floor, three slow breaths. Let the day wait.
  2. Ask one question (1 min). One open question, in your head or aloud: “What does today need from me?” Keep it simple.
  3. Draw one rune (1 min). Reach into the bag without looking and pull one out — or draw one on the page above. Lay it face up.
  4. Look at it (1 min). Say the name, quietly or silently. Let one word come to mind — a picture, a memory. Don't chase meaning.
  5. Carry it (1 min). Set the rune where you'll see it — pocket, windowsill, shelf. That's the whole of it.

You don't need to know what it means. You just need to have looked.

8

Bless the hearth

About 5 minutesWiccanGeneralCan be done quietlyFew supplies

The kitchen is the hearth with cabinets around it — fire, water, and food in one room. A short pause at the sink or stove turns the next chore into something noticed.

You'll need

  • Your kitchen sink or stove
  • Your hands and running water

Steps

  1. Pause (1 min). Stop at the sink or stove before you start. Hands under the water, or on the counter. Feel the temperature.
  2. Name the work (1 min). Silently or aloud, name one thing this meal, wash, or chore will do for your home today — feed, warm, clean, keep.
  3. Do it slowly (2 min). Carry on with the task at half your usual speed. Notice the steam, the weight of the pan, the sound of the water.
  4. Close (1 min). Dry your hands, or turn the flame down. One breath. The work is the blessing.

Ordinary work, noticed, is still ordinary work — and that's enough.

9

Five things, noticed

About 5 minutesGeneral · all pathsCan be done quietlyFew supplies

A gratitude practice with no altar, no words, and nothing to buy — just five things, noticed on purpose, wherever you're sitting.

You'll need

  • Nothing — wherever you're sitting right now works

Steps

  1. Settle (1 min). Sit as you are. Feet down, shoulders down, one slow breath.
  2. See one thing (1 min). Pick one thing you can see. Notice its color, its shape, the light on it. Silently thank it for being there.
  3. Hear and feel (1 min). One sound, near or far. One thing you can feel — the chair under you, the air on your hands, the weight of your own body.
  4. Name one gift (1 min). Bring to mind one thing that isn't in the room — a person, a memory, a plan. Hold it for a breath.
  5. Open your eyes (1 min). If they were closed, open them. Nothing here needed fixing. It was just worth noticing.

Five things, noticed, is a whole practice. Do it at the kitchen table, on the bus, in the garden.

10

A small libation

About 5 minutesHellenicCan be done quietlyFew supplies

In Greek household practice, a libation is a small pour of liquid given to the gods — Hestia receives the first and the last of the offering, the old rule from the hymn to Hestia, kept in every rite. Water is always enough.

You'll need

  • A small cup
  • A little water, wine, or juice
  • An outdoor spot, a plant, or a sink

Steps

  1. Fill the cup (1 min). A little water — a few spoonfuls is plenty. Small is the point.
  2. Stand and settle (1 min). Feet still, hands holding the cup. One slow breath.
  3. Name and pour (2 min). If you have a god in mind, name them, silently or aloud — Hestia first, as keeper of the hearth. Pour the liquid out slowly, onto soil, into a plant, or down the sink. Watch it go.
  4. Close (1 min). One more breath. “Thank you” — said or thought — and step away.

The pour is the offering. Nothing more is owed.

11

Cool water for the Netjeru

About 5 minutesKemeticCan be done quietlyFew supplies

Water was the most basic offering in old Egypt — life in Egypt was the Nile, and the Nile was the gift. A morning pour of cool water to the Netjeru and the ancestors is the smallest honest form of it.

You'll need

  • A cup or small bowl
  • Clean, cool water

Steps

  1. Fill the cup (1 min). Cool water, a small amount. Face east, toward the rising sun.
  2. Settle (1 min). Stand quietly. Let your breath slow.
  3. Pour and name (2 min). Pour a little onto the ground or into a bowl. Speak the names of the gods and ancestors you're honoring — aloud or in your head. “Homage to you, Netjeru of Kemet.”
  4. Close (1 min). Pour the last of it and say, “May Ma'atmah-AHT be established. DuaDOO-ah.”

Dua — praise. The water is given, the day begins.

12

Light a candle for Brigid

About 5 minutesIrishCan be done quietly

BrigidBREE-id, daughter of the Dagda, is the Irish goddess of hearth, forge, and poetry — her flame at Kildare was kept for centuries. A candle and a bowl of water is the smallest way to honor her at home.

You'll need

  • A white or yellow candle in a safe, stable holder
  • A small bowl of water
  • A small offering — bread, honey, or milk

Steps

  1. Set the place (1 min). Candle, water, and offering on the hearth or a table. Light the candle — and never leave it burning unattended.
  2. Watch the flame (1 min). Sit and let your eyes rest on it. One slow breath.
  3. Speak her name (1 min). Say it aloud or silently: “Brigid, keeper of the hearth.” Thank her for the warmth of the house.
  4. Set the offering down (1 min). Bread, honey, or milk beside the flame. Sit a moment in the quiet.
  5. Close (1 min). A short thanks. Snuff the candle before you leave the room.

The flame is tended, the house remembered.

13

Greet the Lares at the lararium

About 5 minutesRomanCan be done quietlyFew supplies

Every Roman home kept a larariumlah-RAH-ree-um — a small shrine where the LaresLAH-rays, the household gods, and the Penatespeh-NAH-tays, keepers of the pantry, were honored daily with a little wine, a little food, and a few words. A shelf and a small dish will do.

You'll need

  • A small cup of wine or water
  • A small portion of your meal — bread, fruit, or whatever you're eating

Steps

  1. Set it aside (1 min). Before you eat, put a small portion of your meal in a dish and pour a little wine or water into the cup.
  2. Stand at the shrine (1 min). At your lararium, or any quiet corner if you have none. Feet still, one slow breath.
  3. Pour and place (2 min). Pour the libation, set the food down, and greet the Lares and Penates by name, aloud or in your head. Thank them for the home and the table.
  4. Close (1 min). Sit quietly a moment, then eat — the meal is the rest of the rite.

The table is shared; the house remembers its own.

14

Leave a bite for the domovoy

About 5 minutesSlavicCan be done quietlyFew supplies

In Slavic homes the domovoy — the house spirit, called Grandfather — lives behind the stove and keeps the household's tally of order and kindness. A crust of bread and a pinch of salt is the old way to feed him.

You'll need

  • A slice of bread
  • A pinch of salt
  • A spoonful of milk or porridge, if you have it

Steps

  1. Cut the bite (1 min). A small slice of bread, sprinkled with salt. A spoonful of milk or porridge on top, if there's any to hand.
  2. Set it by the stove (1 min). On a small plate near the stove or hearth — or a quiet kitchen corner. This is his place.
  3. Speak the greeting (1 min). Softly, or in your head: “Domovoydoh-moh-VOY, father, eat and drink, and let us live in peace.”
  4. Tidy one small thing (1 min). Straighten one thing in the kitchen — a dish put away, a counter wiped. Order is the offering he notices most.
  5. Step away (1 min). Sit quietly a moment with the kitchen. Then step away and leave the plate out overnight; clear it in the morning.

The bite is given; the house keeps its peace.

15

Roots and sky breath

About 5 minutesCeltic · DruidryGeneralCan be done quietlyFew supplies

Druids begin with the land they're actually on — and sometimes the land is just the floor under your feet. A short breath practice, roots down and sky above, for when the day has scattered you.

You'll need

  • Nothing — your feet and your breath

Steps

  1. Stand or sit (1 min). Feet flat on the floor, or sitting with both feet down. Hands loose at your sides.
  2. Feel the floor (1 min). Breathe normally. Notice the weight of your body where it meets the floor — feet, seat, back.
  3. Breathe down (1 min). With each breath out, let that weight settle a little more. No need to picture anything; the floor is doing the work.
  4. Breathe up (1 min). With each breath in, feel the air fill your chest — the sky above, coming in.
  5. Shake it out (1 min). Shake out your hands, roll your shoulders once, and carry on.

You were already here. This just caught up with it.
