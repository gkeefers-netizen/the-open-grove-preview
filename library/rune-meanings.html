Rune Meanings: A Beginner’s Guide

Twenty-four signs, three families, one old alphabet — what the runes are, what each one means, and how to start working with them. Researched and revised November 2026; where a meaning is uncertain, this guide says so.

What runes are

Runes are the letters of the old Germanic alphabets. Each one is a sound — F, U, TH, A, R, K — and each one also carries a name and a meaning. Fehu means cattle. Uruz means the wild ox. Hagalaz means hail. So a rune is never just a letter: it's a letter, a word, and an idea all at once.

The set most people start with is the Elder Futhark — 24 runes, used roughly from the 2nd to the 8th century, named for its first six letters: F-U-TH-A-R-K. It later split into the Younger Futhark (16 runes, Viking-age Scandinavia) and the Anglo-Saxon futhorc (up to 33 runes, England). This guide covers the Elder Futhark, because it's the oldest of the three rows and the one most people work with today. One correction worth making plain: the Vikings themselves used the Younger Futhark, not the Elder — calling the 24-rune row "Viking runes" is a common error.

The earliest runes we can date with confidence are on small objects from the 2nd century — a comb from Vimose in Denmark, reading harja, probably a name (c. 160, though some older literature dates it to the mid-3rd century), and a spearhead from Øvre Stabu in Norway. The oldest carving of the whole 24-rune row in order is on the Kylver stone from Gotland, around the year 400 — though its order differs slightly from the modern standard: it puts Perthro before Eihwaz and ends with Dagaz then Othala, while the bracteates reverse both. In all, some 350–400 Elder Futhark inscriptions survive, most of them short: names, makers' marks, memorials.

You don't need to be Heathen to use runes, and you don't need to believe anything in particular. You need curiosity, a little patience, and a willingness to sit with a sign and let it mean something.

The honest history

Here's what the Grove tells you straight, because the runes attract a lot of fog.

The runes were a writing system first. People carved them on stone, wood, bone, and metal — memorials, makers' marks, ownership, short messages, a little magic worked into the words. They were not a fortune-telling deck. There is no Viking-age book of rune meanings, and no evidence that anyone in the Viking age laid runes out in spreads the way modern readers do.

The rune poems are our main source for the names — and they don't cover the Elder Futhark. Three medieval poems give each rune a name and a short verse: the Old English Rune Poem (probably 8th–9th century, surviving only because George Hickes printed it in 1705 before its manuscript burned in 1731), the Norwegian Rune Poem (from a 13th-century manuscript, itself lost, known through a 17th-century copy), and the Icelandic Rune Poem (oldest manuscript around 1500). There is also a fourth, shorter Swedish poem, and a 9th-century list called the Abecedarium Nordmannicum, which covers the 16 Younger runes, not the Elder row. But the Norwegian and Icelandic poems cover only the 16 runes of the Younger Futhark, and the Old English poem covers 29 Anglo-Saxon runes (the row later grew to 33). No rune poem covers the 24-rune Elder Futhark. The Elder Futhark names in this guide are scholarly reconstructions — best guesses at the Proto-Germanic forms, built by comparing the poems with related words in Old English, Old Norse, and Gothic. Where a reconstruction is shaky, this guide says so in the entry. The poems are still our best evidence; they're just evidence about later alphabets, read backwards.

Runes were used for magic — but not for fortune-telling. The poems themselves show it. In Hávamál, Odin hangs nine nights on a windy tree, wounded with a spear, and takes up the runes (stanzas 138–139), then lists eighteen charms he knows (the Ljóðatal, stanzas 146–163). In Sigrdrífumál, the valkyrie Sigrdrífa teaches the hero Sigurd victory runes to carve on a sword hilt, ale runes for a drinking horn, birth runes, sea runes for a ship, and healing runes cut in bark (stanzas 5–19). In Egils saga, Egil shaves harmful runes off a whalebone and carves healing ones in their place. That is magic done with writing: words and signs carved to make something happen. None of it is reading the future from drawn runes, and no source gives the individual runes fixed divinatory meanings.

The one old account people quote for rune divination is chapter 10 of Tacitus's Germania, written in 98 CE — about sixty years before the earliest runes we can date. Tacitus describes Germanic peoples cutting slips from a fruit-bearing tree, marking them with signs, scattering them on a white cloth, and picking up three, one at a time, to read the answer. He never says the marks were runes. The link between his lots and the runes is a guess. What his account does show is that casting lots for guidance is very old among Germanic peoples — older than the runes themselves.

The modern practice of reading runes is a revival, built in the twentieth century. Its recognizable form comes chiefly from two writers. Guido von List claimed eighteen runes had been revealed to him in a vision in 1902, and published his invented "Armanen" row in 1908, drawing meanings from a misreading of Hávamál. Ralph Blum's The Book of Runes (1982) — sold with a bag of ceramic tiles, modelled substantially on the I Ching — later authors drew the tarot parallel explicitly — using an order of his own devising — is the direct ancestor of the practice most people meet today: short keyword meanings, upright and reversed draws, and the blank rune. The blank rune, the "Wyrd" rune, the reversed readings, and the three-rune spreads all come from that 20th-century line, not from the Viking age. That doesn't make the practice false — it's real practice, made by real people doing it for decades. It just isn't a Viking-age practice, and the Grove won't pretend otherwise.

Two more honest notes. First, the runes were never banned by the Christian church: they went on being carved in Scandinavia well into the Christian Middle Ages, on church walls, baptismal fonts, and gravestones, alongside Latin writing. People stopped using them because the Latin alphabet was more practical for writing on parchment, not because anyone outlawed them. Second, the blank rune — the one some sets sell as "Wyrd," the unknowable — was invented by Blum in 1982. It's not historical. Some readers love it; some leave it out of the bag. Either way is fine, as long as nobody tells you the Vikings used it.

One more honest note, about misuse. Four of these runes — Sowilo (especially doubled, the "SS bolts"), Tiwaz, Algiz, and Othala — have been used in Nazi and white-supremacist symbolism, and the Anti-Defamation League lists them as hate symbols in that use, while noting their innocent use by pagans. Context decides. The Grove uses them only in their proper context — the full alphabet, the poems, the history — never as a lone emblem without it. If you wear or display them, know what company the shapes have kept.

How to read this guide. Each rune below has three layers, and this guide keeps them apart. The name is what the word meant, as far as the poems and related words let us tell. The poems are what the medieval verses actually say — and where the poems disagree with each other, this guide says so, because they often do. The modern reading is what today's readers do with the rune: a prompt for reflection, built in the 20th century from the older parts. The modern readings are the Grove's own working meanings, offered as prompts, not as history.

The three families

The Elder Futhark divides into three groups of eight, called aettir (families). The division itself is old: the Vadstena and Mariedamm bracteates, gold pendants from Sweden dated to the 6th century, mark the row off into three groups of eight with dots. The Vadstena bracteate itself was stolen from the Swedish History Museum in 1938 and never recovered; it survives in drawings and photographs. The Kylver stone (around 400) gives the whole row in order but marks no grouping. The groups were probably a practical way of ordering and remembering the alphabet — they were later used for cipher runes, where a rune is named by its group and its place in the group.

What is not old is what the groups are called. No saga, poem, or inscription names them. The names come from later Icelandic learned tradition — Cleasby and Vigfússon's dictionary of 1874 records Hagals-ætt for the second group — while "Heimdall's aett" and the themes given to each group are fully modern. Modern books call them Freyr's aett, Hagal's aett (also called Heimdall's aett), and Tyr's aett. This guide uses the modern names because they're how everyone talks about the runes now, and gives each family a flavor as a study aid — nothing more:

  • Freyr's aett (runes 1–8): the material world — wealth, strength, the land, joy.
  • Hagal's aett (runes 9–16, also called Heimdall's aett): the hard middle — disruption, need, stillness, and the forces that test you.
  • Tyr's aett (runes 17–24): the human world — justice, growth, partnership, home.

Learn one family at a time. Eight runes is a good week's work.

Freyr's aett — the world you live in

Fehu ᚠ — F — cattle, wealth. The name is secure: Old English feoh, Old Norse fé, both meaning cattle and the wealth cattle were. The poems agree on the word and disagree on the feeling: the Old English poem calls wealth a comfort to everyone, but says a man must share it freely if he wants glory; the Norse poems call it a source of strife among kinsmen. Modern reading: movable wealth — what you own and what it can do. Not riches for their own sake; cattle were food, trade, and survival. Fehu asks: what do you have, and are you using it well? Upright, it speaks of abundance and flow; it can also warn against clinging to what should move on.

Uruz ᚢ — U — the wild ox. The reconstructed name is *ūruz, "aurochs" — the great wild ox of the old forests, extinct since 1627. The case for it is good: the Old English poem describes the aurochs at length — proud, great-horned, a savage beast that fights with its horns — and the Gothic letter name was urus. But the later Norse poems use the same sound for something else entirely: the Icelandic poem calls úr the weeping of clouds, the ruin of the hay harvest, the shepherd's hatred — drizzle; the Norwegian poem calls it slag from bad iron. Meanings shifted as the language changed. Modern reading: raw strength, health, and vitality — the energy to act and to endure. It asks: where is your strength, and what are you spending it on?

Thurisaz ᚦ — TH — the giant; in England, the thorn. Two traditions, two names, and scholars don't agree which is older. The Norse poems call the rune þurs, a giant — a hostile being of myth, "the torment of women, dweller in the cliffs." The Old English poem calls it þorn, a thorn, "exceedingly sharp, an evil thing for any thegn to touch." The reconstructed name *þurisaz, "giant," is the usual handbook form, but it should be read as a reconstruction from one branch of the evidence. One confusion to avoid: Thurisaz has nothing to do with Thor. The giant is Thor's opponent, not Thor himself. Modern reading: defense and the force that clears the way — sometimes by breaking things. A thorn is small and it stops you. It asks: what needs to be kept out, and what needs to be cut through?

Ansuz ᚨ — A — a god; in England, the mouth. The reconstructed name *ansuz means "god" — one of the Æsir, the family of Odin and Thor. The poems hear that name three different ways. The Icelandic poem names the god outright: aged Gautr, prince of Ásgarðr, lord of Valhöll — all names for Odin. The Old English poem turns it into ōs, the mouth: "the source of all language, a pillar of wisdom and a comfort to wise men." The Norwegian poem hears óss as an estuary, "the way of most journeys." Modern readers join the Icelandic Odin to the English speech, and that joining is what Ansuz means in practice today. The "breath" in some modern books is the same kind of joining: Odin gave breath (önd) to the first humans in Völuspá, and the rune has come to carry it. Modern reading: the word spoken well — counsel, a message, a song, a story that lands true. It asks: what needs saying, and are you listening?

Raidho ᚱ — R — riding, the journey. The name is secure: Old English rād, Old Norse reið, riding. The Old English poem notes that riding is easy for the one who sits in the hall and hard for the one on the horse. Modern reading: movement with a purpose — a journey, a plan carried out, the right order of things. It asks: are you moving, and is the road the right one?

Kenaz ᚲ — K — the sore; in England, the torch. This rune is the clearest case in the row of a meaning that split between traditions, and this guide won't smooth it over. The Norwegian and Icelandic poems call it kaun, a sore or ulcer — "fatal to children," "the bane of children," "the house of rotting flesh." The Old English poem calls it cēn, a torch, "known to every living man by its pale, bright flame," burning where princes sit within. Philologists generally favor *kauną, "sore," as the older form; the torch reading belongs to the English branch. The modern reading of Kenaz as knowledge, craft, and the light you make yourself comes from the English poem only. Modern reading: a torch in the dark — skill learned by doing, the smith's fire and the poet's spark. It asks: what do you know how to make, and what does the light show you? Keep the other half in mind too: the same heat that lights a hall can fester.

Gebo ᚷ — G — gift. The name is secure: Old English gyfu, Old Norse gjöf, a gift. Modern reading: a gift given and a gift returned — exchange, partnership, the balance between giving and taking. Gebo says nothing is free and nothing is owed forever: the gift creates the bond. It asks: what are you giving, and what are you receiving?

Wunjo ᚹ — W — joy. The name is secure: Old English wyn, joy, bliss. The Old English poem says joy is had by one who knows few troubles and has prosperity and a good house. The Norse poems have no stanza for it — the Younger Futhark dropped this rune. Modern reading: the banner, the glad heart, harmony after the work is done — fellowship, comfort, a place at the table. It asks: where is the joy in your life, and are you letting yourself feel it?

Hagal's aett — the hard middle

Hagalaz ᚺ — H — hail. The name is secure: Old English hægl, Old Norse hagall, hail. The Old English poem calls hail the whitest of grains; the Norwegian and Icelandic poems call it the coldest — and all three agree it ruins the crop. Modern reading: disruption from outside your control. Hail falls and ruins the crop — a natural force, no one's fault, and no arguing with it. It asks: what has been broken open, and what can grow in the clearing?

Nauthiz ᚾ — N — need. The name is secure: Old English nȳd, Old Norse nauð, need, distress, constraint. Modern reading: necessity, the friction that forces invention — the rune of doing without, and of finding out what you actually need. It asks: what is the lack teaching you?

Isa ᛁ — I — ice. The name is secure: Old English īs, Old Norse íss, ice. Modern reading: stillness. Ice stops the river, and sometimes stopping is the only move — waiting without struggling, holding your place until the thaw. It asks: what needs to be left alone for now?

Jera ᛃ — J — year, harvest. The name is secure: Old English gēr, Old Norse ár, year — and in the Norse poems, a good year, plenty. Modern reading: the turning year, the harvest after the work — the slow reward. What you sowed comes back, in its season, not yours. It asks: what did you plant, and are you patient enough to reap it?

Eihwaz ᛇ — EI — the yew. The name is secure: Old English ēoh, the yew. The Old English poem — the only poem that has this rune — calls it "a tree with rough bark, hard and fast in the earth, supported by its roots, a guardian of flame, a joy upon an estate." The yew is evergreen, long-lived, and its wood made bows. Modern reading: endurance — what bends without breaking. It asks: what bends without breaking in you? One thing this guide will not say: that Eihwaz is the world-tree. Yggdrasil is an ash in the sources, and the yew-as-world-tree is a modern suggestion, not something the old texts say.

Perthro ᛈ — P — meaning unknown. This is the honest one, and the rune poems make it plain. The Old English poem — the only poem that has this rune — calls peorð "ever a source of play and laughter to the proud, where warriors sit in the beer-hall, happy together." What peorð actually was, nobody knows. The most widely accepted reading, going back to Bruce Dickins in 1915, is a lot-cup or dice-box — the container the lots were shaken in — and the rune's shape, like a cup tipped on its side, supports it. But a gaming piece, pear-wood, and even a womb have all been proposed, and the name itself is a modern reconstruction. "Unknown" is the most honest scholarly answer. Modern reading: mystery, chance, fate — the cup shaken and the lot cast. It asks the question the rune itself asks: can you live with not knowing?

Algiz ᛉ — Z — the elk, probably; the name is a convention. This rune's name is the least certain in the row, and the story is worth telling. The sound it stands for, z, never begins a word, so the rune could not be named the way the others were. The name algiz, "elk," is a 19th-century convention: Jacob Grimm suggested it in 1821 from the Old English rune poem, which calls the rune eolh-secg — "elk-sedge," a marsh plant, not an elk standing in a field. Whether the rune was ever named for an elk at all is debated; it may have been named for the sedge. The Norse descendant of this rune, turned upside down, became ýr — the yew. The "protection" reading is modern: readers saw the shape as raised arms, or antlers, and made it a rune of defense and of reaching upward. Modern reading: protection, and the higher self that watches over you. It asks: what protects you, and what are you reaching for?

Sowilo ᛊ — S — the sun. The name is secure: Old English sigel, Old Norse sól, the sun. The Old English poem calls the sun "a hope to seafarers". Modern reading: victory, clarity, wholeness — the light after the storm in this family, the strength that comes from seeing clearly. It asks: where is the light, and are you standing in it?

Tyr's aett — the human world

Tiwaz ᛏ — T — Tyr, the god. The name is secure: Old English tīr, Old Norse týr. The Norse poems remember Tyr as the one-handed god — the god who put his hand in the wolf Fenrir's mouth as the price of binding him, told in the Prose Edda. The Old English poem makes tīr a guiding star that keeps faith with princes and never fails on its course over the mists of night. Modern reading: justice, courage, and victory won honorably. It asks: what is right, and will you stand for it?

Berkana ᛒ — B — the birch. The name is secure: Old Norse bjarkan, the birch. The Norwegian poem says the birch has the greenest leaves of any shrub. Modern reading: growth, renewal, and new beginnings — the green shoot after winter, the first tree of spring, pale and quick to grow. It asks: what is growing in you?

Ehwaz ᛖ — E — the horse. The name is secure: Old English eoh, the horse — the only poem that has this rune. The Old English poem calls the horse "a joy to princes in the presence of warriors, a steed proud of its hooves, and ever a comfort to the restless." The "two horses running together" you will see in some modern books is a modern image, not something the poem says; the poem describes one horse, and a rider's bond with it. Modern reading: trust and partnership — movement made together. It asks: who are you riding with, and do you trust them?

Mannaz ᛗ — M — man, humanity. The name is secure: Old English mann, Old Norse maðr, a human being. The poems call man the delight of his kin and an addition to the earth. Modern reading: the self seen clearly, and the help we give each other. It asks: who are you, really, and who helps you be that?

Laguz ᛚ — L — water. The name is secure: Old English lagu, Old Norse lögr, water, lake. The Old English poem describes the sea as seeming without end to men who venture on it, and the waves terrifying them. Modern reading: intuition, feeling, and going with the current instead of against it — the lake, the sea, the flow. It asks: what is the water telling you, and can you let yourself drift a little?

Ingwaz ᛜ — NG — Ing, a god or hero. The name is ingwaz, after Ing — and the only poem that has this rune is the Old English one, which tells a strange little story: "Ing was first seen among the East Danes, until he later went back over the waves, his wagon running after him." Ing is usually identified with Yngvi-Freyr, the Norse god of fertility, and the fertility reading follows from that identification — but the poem itself says nothing about seeds or fertility, and calling Ingwaz a fertility symbol is a modern association, not a historical one. Modern reading: potential held in stillness — the seed in the ground, waiting for its season, the quiet power of what hasn't happened yet. It asks: what is ripening in you?

Dagaz ᛞ — D — day, dawn. The name is secure: Old English dæg, Old Norse dagr, day. The Old English poem calls day the Lord's messenger, dear to men, a joy and a hope to rich and poor, useful to all. Modern reading: the dawn breaking — clarity, awakening, the turning point where dark becomes light. It asks: what has dawned on you?

Othala ᛟ — O — inheritance, home. The name is secure: Old English ōþel, Old Norse óðal, inherited land, ancestral property. Modern reading: the ancestral land, what is passed down — the home, the name, the belonging; legacy and the ground you stand on. It asks: what did your people give you, and what will you pass on?

How to begin

You don't need a set to start. You don't even need to know all twenty-four. Here's the way in:

1. Pick one rune. Read its meaning above. Carry it with you for a day — write it on your hand, or on a scrap of paper in your pocket. Notice where it shows up.

2. Learn one family a week. Eight runes, one aett, seven days. By the end of three weeks you'll know the whole Futhark, and you'll know it the way that sticks — by living with it, not by memorizing a list.

3. Try a three-rune draw. Shake your runes in a bag (or use the Grove's rune translator to draw them). Lay out three: the first is the situation, the second is the action, the third is the outcome. The three-rune spread itself is modern practice — Tacitus does describe Germanic diviners picking up three marked slips, one at a time, but he never says the marks were runes, and he wrote about sixty years before the earliest securely dated runes. Don't ask the runes to decide for you — ask them to show you what you're not seeing. Then decide for yourself.

4. Make a set, or buy one. A set of stones or wooden discs with the runes painted on is easy to make and means more for the making. If you buy one, choose it the way you'd choose anything that matters — because it feels right in your hand.

Runes in the shop — and one honest distinction

The Grove's rune designs live in the shop — including the rune mug, which carries the runes around its side for your morning coffee. You'll also see the Vegvísir on the site and in the shop. Here's the distinction, because it matters: the Vegvísir is a stave — a later Icelandic wayfinding sign — not a rune. Its earliest known appearance is in the Huld manuscript, copied by Geir Vigfússon in Akureyri in 1860, which says that whoever carries the sign will not lose their way in storms or bad weather, even when the way is not known. It belongs to the real tradition of Icelandic magical staves, the galdrastafir — but it isn't one of the twenty-four, and it isn't Viking-age. The Grove won't sell it to you as one.

Questions people ask

Are runes magic? They were used for magic sometimes — carved into weapons, written into charms, spoken as spells. Hávamál tells how Odin won them by hanging nine nights on a tree; Sigrdrífumál teaches victory runes for a sword hilt, ale runes for a drinking horn, and healing runes cut in bark; Egils saga shows a man shaving harmful runes off a bone and carving healing ones in their place. But mostly they were letters. The magic in runes today is the same as the magic in any focused attention: you look at a sign, you think hard about what it means, and something in you shifts. That's real enough without claiming more.

Do I need to be Heathen to use runes? No. Heathens honor the Norse gods and the runes are part of their world — but the runes were a writing system before they were anyone's religious symbol, and plenty of people from other paths work with them. Respect the source, and you're welcome.

Can I use runes if I'm Wiccan, or Druid, or solitary? Yes. Runes travel well. The Grove's own practice is mixed — Heathenry leaning, a little Wiccan for flavor — and the runes sit comfortably in it.

What about the blank rune? It was invented in 1982 by the writer Ralph Blum, in The Book of Runes, as "Odin's rune" — the unknowable. Later books often call it the "Wyrd" rune. It's not historical, and the old rune poems don't mention it. Blum's book is also where the familiar system of upright and reversed rune readings comes from — he modelled it substantially on the I Ching, and later authors drew the tarot parallel explicitly. Blum himself later said the blank rune came from a home-made set and two photocopied sheets he acquired in England in 1976, and called it a "later innovation". Some readers love the blank rune; some leave it out. Either is fine — just know where it came from.

Do runes have reversed meanings? Some readers use them — a rune drawn upside-down means its shadow side. But there is no historical evidence for reversed readings; the convention comes from Blum's 1982 book, and it has no source in any runic tradition. Others don't use reversals at all, because most runes look the same upside-down anyway (Isa is a straight line; Jera and Sowilo and Ingwaz don't change at all — nor do Gebo, Dagaz, and Eihwaz when turned 180°). The Grove's advice: learn the upright meanings first. The shadow side of a rune is already in its story — Fehu's greed, Thurisaz's destruction, Nauthiz's need. You don't need to turn the stone over to find it.

Keep going

Twenty-four runes, three families, one old alphabet — and a lifetime to learn what they mean to you. Start with one. Carry it for a day. See where it shows up.

Corrections of fact (v1 said it wrong):

  • v1 said the Elder Futhark is "the set the rune poems describe." Wrong. No rune poem covers the 24-rune Elder Futhark: the Norwegian and Icelandic poems cover the 16-rune Younger Futhark, the Old English poem covers 29 Anglo-Saxon runes. The Elder Futhark names are scholarly reconstructions from those poems and related words. v2 says this plainly and says where reconstructions are shaky.
  • v1 gave the aettir god-names (Freyr's, Heimdall's, Tyr's) and their themes as if they were old. The threefold division is old (Vadstena and Mariedamm bracteates, 6th century), but the names come from later Icelandic learned tradition and the themes are a modern convention. v2 says so, and uses "Hagal's aett (also called Heimdall's aett)" for the second family, since Hagal's is the more common modern naming.
  • v1 said Kenaz is "torch, fire." The Norse poems say the opposite: kaun, a sore or ulcer, "fatal to children." The torch comes from the Old English poem only, and philologists generally favor kauną (sore) as the older form. v2 tells the split.
  • v1 said Uruz is "the wild ox" without qualification. The aurochs reading is the best-supported reconstruction (Old English poem, Gothic letter name urus), but the Norse poems use the same sound for drizzle (Icelandic) and slag (Norwegian). v2 notes the split.
  • v1 said Algiz is "the elk." The elk name is a 19th-century convention from Jacob Grimm's 1821 reading of the Old English poem's eolh-secg ("elk-sedge," a marsh plant), and is probably not the historical name. The Norse descendant ýr means yew. v2 tells the story.
  • v1 said Eihwaz is "the world-tree that holds everything together." Yggdrasil is an ash in the sources; the yew-as-world-tree is a modern suggestion. Cut; v2 keeps the yew, the bow wood, and the endurance.
  • v1 said Ehwaz is "two horses running together." The Old English poem describes one horse; the twin-horses image is modern. Cut; v2 keeps the partnership reading but labels it modern.
  • v1 said Ingwaz is "Ing, the god of the seed in the ground." The Old English poem only says Ing was seen among the East Danes and went east over the waves with his wagon. The fertility reading follows from identifying Ing with Yngvi-Freyr, and the "seed" image is modern devotional language. v2 says so.

Softenings (v1 stated as certain what is uncertain):

  • Perthro: v1 said "the dice cup" as fact. The meaning is genuinely unknown; lot-cup/dice-box is the most widely accepted reading (Dickins, 1915), but gaming piece, pear-wood, and womb have all been proposed. v2 says "meaning unknown" in the heading.
  • Thurisaz: v1 merged "thorn, the giant" into one phrase. They are two names from two traditions (Norse þurs, giant; English þorn, thorn), and which is older is not known. v2 separates them. Also added: Thurisaz has nothing to do with Thor.
  • Ansuz: v1 merged "Odin, breath, the mouth." The Icelandic poem names Odin; the Old English poem says mouth/source of speech; the Norwegian poem says estuary. The "breath" link (Odin giving önd) is a modern joining. v2 separates the three and labels the joining as modern.

Additions (things v1 left out that thorough research supports):

  • Earliest evidence: Vimose comb (c. 160, harja; some older literature dates it to the mid-3rd century), Øvre Stabu spearhead, Kylver stone (c. 400, full row in order), 350–400 surviving inscriptions.
  • The fourth (Swedish) rune poem and the 9th-century Abecedarium Nordmannicum.
  • The Eddic evidence for rune magic: Hávamál 138–139 and the Ljóðatal (146–163), Sigrdrífumál 5–19, Egils saga ch. 72.
  • Tacitus, Germania 10 (98 CE): lot-casting with marked slips on a white cloth, three slips picked up one at a time — and the honest limit that he never says the marks were runes.
  • The modern revival's actual history: Guido von List (vision 1902, published 1908, Armanen runes) and Ralph Blum (1982, The Book of Runes, modelled substantially on the I Ching; later authors drew the tarot parallel explicitly; source of the blank rune and of upright/reversed readings).
  • The runes were never banned by the church; they were carved on churches and gravestones into the Christian Middle Ages.
  • The Vegvísir's first appearance: Huld manuscript, Geir Vigfússon, Akureyri, 1860, with its instruction quoted.

Claims checked and kept as written: Elder Futhark 24 runes, 2nd–8th century; Younger Futhark 16 runes, Viking age; futhorc up to 33 runes; Fehu, Raidho, Gebo, Wunjo, Hagalaz, Nauthiz, Isa, Jera, Sowilo, Tiwaz, Berkana, Mannaz, Laguz, Dagaz, Othala name meanings; the blank rune as Blum's 1982 invention; the symmetry point on reversed runes (Isa, Jera, Sowilo, Ingwaz, Gebo, Dagaz, and Eihwaz unchanged upside down); Vegvísir as a later Icelandic stave, not a rune.

Claims I could not verify (cut or qualified):

  • "Thurisaz is Thor's rune" — not claimed in v1, but the confusion is common; v2 explicitly rules it out.
  • Any fixed historical meaning for Perthro — none exists; v2 leaves it unknown.
  • A historical name for Algiz — none is established; v2 calls the elk name a convention.
  • A single original meaning for Kenaz or Uruz — the traditions disagree; v2 presents both sides rather than picking one.
  • "The runes were banned" — no evidence; v2 states the opposite.
